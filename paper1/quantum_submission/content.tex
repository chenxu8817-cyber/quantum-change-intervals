\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Quantum change-point inference asks where an ordered source changes from one
state-generating process to another. Unlike a classical observer, the receiver
may retain the systems and measure the entire sequence collectively.
Early Bayesian work considered unknown pure states and two possible change
locations \cite{akimoto2011}. For a single permanent change, collective
minimum-error discrimination has a nonzero asymptotic success probability and
can outperform online local strategies \cite{sentis2016}; exact and online
unambiguous-identification protocols were subsequently developed in
Refs.~\cite{sentis2017,sentis2018}. An adaptive Bayesian online protocol was
also demonstrated experimentally with photonic state sequences
\cite{yu2018}. Separate formulations optimize different operational risks.
Quickest and sequential quantum change detection trade false-alarm control
against detection delay \cite{fanizza2023,zecchin2026}. Hamiltonian change
points are finite-candidate quantum process-discrimination problems under a
minimum-error criterion \cite{nakahira2023}, whereas the 2026 channel result
studies unambiguous process discrimination and hence permits an inconclusive
outcome \cite{nakahira2026}. The literature also includes general Gram-matrix
semidefinite programs, multiple changes, isolated anomalies, and boundaries
between quantum domains
\cite{mohan2023,skotiniotis2024,llorens2024,llorens2025}.

Here the change is transient: the source returns to its reference state after
one nonempty contiguous anomalous block. We refer to this
interval-constrained family as a \emph{quantum change interval}. For a
sequence of length \(n\) and an interval \(I=[a,b]\), the corresponding
hypothesis is

\[
|\Phi_{a,b}\rangle
=|0\rangle^{\otimes(a-1)}|\psi\rangle^{\otimes(b-a+1)}
|0\rangle^{\otimes(n-b)}.
\]

When the length \(i=b-a+1\) is known, only the translation \(a\) is inferred;
when the length is unknown, both endpoints are inferred.
Figure~\ref{fig:model-geometry} summarizes this returning geometry and the
collective exact-localization task.

The model is related to established quantum tasks through exact restrictions.
The boundary slice \(b=n\) is the permanent quantum change-point family
\cite{sentis2016,sentis2017,sentis2018}. In the two-change notation of
Mohan--Sikora--Upadhyay \cite{mohan2023}, the present candidates are obtained
by setting \(N=n\), \(c_1=a-1\), \(c_2=b\), and choosing the initial, middle,
and final states as \(\lvert0\rangle,\lvert\psi\rangle,\lvert0\rangle\),
respectively. After the phase convention used below, their three pairwise
overlaps specialize to \(c,c,1\). Thus their piecewise two-change Gram kernel
contains the returning family, while the nonempty-interval restriction imposes
\(c_1<c_2\). For fixed length \(i\), the interval candidates are the
consecutive \(k=i\) subensemble of multi-anomaly detection
\cite{llorens2024}; the containment is strict for \(1<i<n\), whereas the
hypothesis sets coincide at \(i=1\) and \(i=n\). The unknown-length family is
the union of the consecutive subensembles. Quantum edge detection locates one
boundary between unknown pure-state domains and proves an SRM-based protocol
asymptotically optimal \cite{llorens2025}. It is a single-boundary geometric
analogue: the present model instead ties two endpoints to one interval, uses
calibrated local states, and fixes both exterior domains to
\(\lvert0\rangle\).

The unknown-length problem has a specific obstruction that is hidden by the
two-endpoint notation. For intersecting intervals, the overlap kernel depends
only on the endpoint displacement and agrees with a separable two-dimensional
Toeplitz kernel. For separated intervals, however, the physical overlap
contains an additional correction determined by the gap between the two
intervals. Thus the exact physical Gram matrix is neither a global
two-dimensional Toeplitz compression nor an element of a transitive
association scheme. A direct multilevel Szeg{\H o} theorem therefore does not
solve the problem. The proof deletes a short-interval sector whose prior mass
vanishes, compares the retained long-interval Gram matrix in operator norm
with a separable convolution kernel on a triangular Følner sequence, and
obtains matching SRM and optimum limits through local-diagonal functional
calculus and a Gram upper bound. A prior-mass sandwich transfers the optimum
to all intervals, while principal-submatrix interlacing, including the
essential retained-family factor, transfers the square-root trace and hence
the SRM of the full physical family.

Throughout this paper, \emph{exact localization} refers to the zero--one
reward: an outcome is successful only when the reported interval
\(\widehat I\) equals the true interval \(I\). The minimum-error POVM reports
an interval on every run and may err. In unambiguous exact identification,
every conclusive report must be correct and an inconclusive outcome is allowed
\cite{sentis2017,llorens2024}. Certified-answer discrimination instead bounds
the distance between a reported and true ordered label, interpolating between
unambiguous and minimum-error criteria \cite{martinez2019}. We study the
minimum-error zero--one reward, including its joint extension with a
no-anomaly label.

One operational scenario considers a device emitting a batch of quantum
probes or carriers during a transient coherent disturbance. The model assumes
independent single-system outputs, a calibrated pure anomalous state
\(\lvert\psi\rangle\), an abrupt switch from \(\lvert0\rangle\), a state that
remains constant throughout the anomalous block, and a perfect return to
\(\lvert0\rangle\). A receiver may retain the complete batch coherently and
apply an unrestricted global POVM to estimate the onset and duration. We treat
this as an ultimate collective-measurement benchmark. Correlated or mixed
outputs, gradual switching, imperfect recovery, unknown fault states, and
restricted storage or readout architectures require different models.

The same returning geometry appears in classical statistics. Epidemic
alternatives and changed-segment models place a temporary distributional
change on an unknown contiguous
interval \cite{levin1985,yao1993}, while scan methods cover spatial or
graph-structured changed regions \cite{walther2010,chen2015}. Those works
optimize classical test statistics and type-I-error or power criteria. Here
the observations are noncommuting quantum hypotheses, arbitrary collective
POVMs are allowed, and the target is the exact Bayes localization probability.
Table~\ref{tab:literature} summarizes the resulting distinctions.

We use \emph{quantum change interval} as a descriptive name for this returning
contiguous ensemble, a structured instance of multiple quantum changes and
anomalous-site identification. Toeplitz and circulant asymptotics, including
multilevel spectral-distribution theorems, provide the matrix-analytic
background \cite{gray2006,tilli1998,tyrtyshnikov1998}. The contribution is the
combination of exact asymptotic Bayes laws, a Toeplitz/Følner reduction for the
non-Toeplitz physical kernel, and an exceptional-sector Gram theorem that
transfers both the optimum and SRM limit to the full physical family. This
transfer mechanism applies to other structured Gram ensembles satisfying its
bulk-comparison hypotheses. More precisely:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  For fixed known length \(i\), the optimal and SRM success probabilities converge to a one-dimensional Toeplitz integral, with an \(O(N^{-1/2})\) bound on their difference.
\item
  If \(i=i_n\to\infty\) and the number \(N_n=n-i_n+1\) of admissible translations also diverges, both probabilities converge to \(p_1(c^2)\), with no assumption on \(i_n/N_n\).
\item
  Under the prior uniform on all nonempty intervals, both probabilities
  converge to the prior-specific law \(p_1(c)^2\). The change from
  \(p_1(c^2)\) to \(p_1(c)^2\) reflects the replacement of one translation
  coordinate by two endpoint coordinates together with the elementary overlap
  change from \(c^2\) to \(c\).
\item
  If the no-anomaly prior \(\pi_0\in(0,1)\) is fixed and the conditional
  localization limit is \(L\), the optimal joint detection-and-localization
  success probability converges to \(\pi_0+(1-\pi_0)L\). This item makes no
  augmented weighted-SRM claim.
\end{enumerate}

All results assume independent outputs, abrupt switching, a calibrated pure
anomalous state, and a perfect return to \(\lvert0\rangle\). The overlap \(c\)
is fixed as \(n\to\infty\), and \(\pi_0\) is fixed whenever \(H_0\) is present.
The stated uniform priors and arbitrary collective POVMs define the decision
problems. Section~\ref{discussion-and-limitations} discusses extensions beyond
one interval, fixed states and overlaps, and unrestricted coherent readout.

\section{Model and discrimination preliminaries}\label{model-and-discrimination-preliminaries}

\subsection{Phase convention and Gram matrices}\label{phase-convention-and-gram-matrices}

Write

\[
\langle0|\psi\rangle=ce^{i\varphi},\qquad 0\le c\le1,
\qquad r=c^2.
\]

Replacing \(|\psi\rangle\) by \(e^{-i\varphi}|\psi\rangle\) changes each candidate vector only by a hypothesis-dependent global phase. Equivalently, the original and phase-fixed Gram matrices are related by a diagonal unitary conjugation. Minimum-error and SRM success probabilities are unchanged. We therefore use

\[
\langle0|\psi\rangle=c\ge0
\]

without loss of generality.

Unless a theorem explicitly introduces a sequence \(i=i_n\), every
asymptotic statement is taken as \(n\to\infty\) with \(c\) (and hence
\(r=c^2\)) fixed. In Section~\ref{joint-detection-and-exact-localization},
the no-anomaly prior \(\pi_0\in(0,1)\) is also fixed rather than scaled with
\(n\).

For an ensemble of \(M\) normalized pure states \(\{|\phi_j\rangle\}_{j=1}^M\) with equal priors, let

\[
G_{jk}=\langle\phi_j|\phi_k\rangle.
\]

We write \(P_{\rm opt}(G)\) for the minimum-error optimum over all POVMs
\cite{helstrom1976,holevo1973,yuen1975,barnett2009} and

\[
P_{\rm SRM}(G)=\frac1M\sum_{j=1}^M(\sqrt G)_{jj}^2
\]

for the square-root-measurement success probability
\cite{hausladen1994,eldar2001,dallapozza2015}. If \(G\) is singular,
\(G^{-1/2}\) is understood on the support of \(G\), and the POVM is completed
arbitrarily on its orthogonal complement; this does not change any ensemble
success probability. The elementary trace bound

\begin{equation}\label{eq:trace-srm-bound}
\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}\sqrt G}{M}\right)^2
\le P_{\rm SRM}(G)\le P_{\rm opt}(G)
\end{equation}

will be used repeatedly.

\subsection{Stability under Gram perturbations}\label{stability-under-gram-perturbations}

\begin{lemma}[Operator-norm Gram stability]\label{lem:gram-stability}
Let \(G,H\in\mathbb C^{M\times M}\) be positive semidefinite with unit diagonal. Then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:opt-gram-stability}
|P_{\rm opt}(G)-P_{\rm opt}(H)|
\le \|\sqrt G-\sqrt H\|_{\rm op}
\le \|G-H\|_{\rm op}^{1/2}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{align}\label{eq:srm-gram-stability}
&|P_{\rm SRM}(G)-P_{\rm SRM}(H)| \notag\\
&\qquad\le2\|\sqrt G-\sqrt H\|_{\rm op}
\le2\|G-H\|_{\rm op}^{1/2}.
\end{align}

If \(G,H\ge \mu I\) for some \(\mu>0\), then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:sqrt-lipschitz}
\|\sqrt G-\sqrt H\|_{\rm op}
\le \frac{\|G-H\|_{\rm op}}{2\sqrt\mu}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Use the canonical Gram realizations \(|x_j\rangle=\sqrt G e_j\) and
\(|y_j\rangle=\sqrt H e_j\). Their norms are one because the Gram matrices
have unit diagonal, and the trace distance between the corresponding pure
states is at most \(\|x_j-y_j\|\). Apply an optimal POVM for either ensemble
to the other and average over \(j\) to obtain the first inequality in
\eqref{eq:opt-gram-stability}. The second is the operator Hölder inequality
for positive square roots. Since
\(0\le(\sqrt G)_{jj},(\sqrt H)_{jj}\le1\), the scalar bound
\(|a^2-b^2|\le2|a-b|\) gives \eqref{eq:srm-gram-stability}. For the final
claim, \(X=\sqrt G-\sqrt H\) solves

\[
\sqrt G X+X\sqrt H=G-H.
\]

The inverse Sylvester operator has norm at most \((2\sqrt\mu)^{-1}\), which proves \eqref{eq:sqrt-lipschitz}.
\end{proof}

\section{A known interval length}\label{a-known-interval-length}

Let the anomalous interval have known length \(i\ge1\). There are

\[
N=n-i+1
\]

admissible starting points, and the equal-prior candidates are

\begin{align*}
|\Phi_a^{(i)}\rangle
={}&|0\rangle^{\otimes(a-1)}|\psi\rangle^{\otimes i}
|0\rangle^{\otimes(n-a-i+1)}, \\
&a=1,\ldots,N.
\end{align*}

Their exact Gram matrix is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-gram}
(G_{N,i})_{ab}=r^{\min(|a-b|,i)}.
\end{equation}

Define

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-symbol}
f_{i,r}(\theta)
=1-r^i+2\sum_{d=1}^{i-1}(r^d-r^i)\cos(d\theta).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Fixed-length limit}\label{fixed-length-limit}

\begin{theorem}[Fixed-length localization]\label{thm:fixed-length}
For every fixed integer \(i\ge1\) and \(0\le r\le1\),

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-limit}
\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{\rm opt}(G_{N,i})
=\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{\rm SRM}(G_{N,i})
=P_\infty(i,r),
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-integral}
P_\infty(i,r)
=\left[
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{f_{i,r}(\theta)}\,d\theta
\right]^2.
\end{equation}

For fixed \(i\) and \(0<r<1\),

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fixed-gap-rate}
0\le P_{\rm opt}(G_{N,i})-P_{\rm SRM}(G_{N,i})
=O_{i,r}(N^{-1/2}).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put \(q=r^i\) and decompose

\[
G_{N,i}=qJ_N+B_{N,i},
\]

where \(B_{N,i}=T_N(f_{i,r})\) has entries

\[
(B_{N,i})_{ab}=
\begin{cases}
r^{|a-b|}-r^i,&|a-b|<i,\\
0,&|a-b|\ge i.
\end{cases}
\]

Choose a local basis with \(|\psi\rangle=\sqrt r\,|0\rangle+\sqrt{1-r}\,|1\rangle\). After removing the common all-zero component, each candidate has a basis component, with squared amplitude \((1-r)^i\), that occurs in no other candidate. Hence

\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual-spectral-gap}
B_{N,i}\ge(1-r)^iI_N.
\end{equation}

The corresponding infinite residual convolution operator has symbol \(f_{i,r}\), so \eqref{eq:residual-spectral-gap}, applied to arbitrarily long finitely supported vectors, implies

\begin{equation}\label{eq:symbol-spectral-gap}
f_{i,r}(\theta)\ge(1-r)^i.
\end{equation}

For \(N\ge2i\), let \(\widetilde B_{N,i}\) be the circulant matrix with the same local coefficients and set

\[
C_{N,i}=r^iJ_N+\widetilde B_{N,i}.
\]

Its eigenvalues away from the uniform Fourier mode are the sampled values \(f_{i,r}(2\pi k/N)\); the uniform mode receives the additional positive rank-one contribution. Thus \(C_{N,i}\) is a positive definite, unit-diagonal circulant Gram matrix. The matrices \(B_{N,i}\) and \(\widetilde B_{N,i}\) differ only in the upper-right and lower-left wrap-around corner blocks.

To make the boundary estimate explicit, set

\begin{align*}
b_d&=r^d-r^i,\\
E_{N,d}&=\sum_{a=1}^{d}e_a e_{N-d+a}^*,
\qquad 1\le d<i.
\end{align*}

Here \(E_{N,d}\) is a rank-\(d\) partial permutation from the last \(d\)
coordinates to the first \(d\) coordinates, and

\begin{equation}\label{eq:corner-partial-permutation}
\widetilde B_{N,i}-B_{N,i}
=\sum_{d=1}^{i-1}b_d(E_{N,d}+E_{N,d}^*).
\end{equation}

For \(N\ge2i\), the initial and terminal coordinate subspaces in
\eqref{eq:corner-partial-permutation} are disjoint. Consequently
\(\|E_{N,d}+E_{N,d}^*\|_1=2d\). Moreover, the whole difference maps between
the span of the first \(i-1\) coordinates and the span of the last \(i-1\)
coordinates. The triangle inequality and this two-corner support therefore give

\begin{equation}\label{eq:circulant-trace-perturbation}
\|G_{N,i}-C_{N,i}\|_1
\le K_{i,r}:=2\sum_{d=1}^{i-1}d(r^d-r^i),
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:circulant-rank-perturbation}
\operatorname{rank}(G_{N,i}-C_{N,i})\le2(i-1).
\end{equation}

The Sylvester integral gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:sqrt-trace-perturbation}
\|\sqrt{G_{N,i}}-\sqrt{C_{N,i}}\|_1
\le\frac{K_{i,r}}{2(1-r)^{i/2}}=O_{i,r}(1).
\end{equation}

Because \(C_{N,i}\) and \(\sqrt{C_{N,i}}\) are circulant, the diagonal of \(\sqrt{C_{N,i}}\) is constant. If

\[
\beta_N=\frac1N\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{C_{N,i}},
\]

then the SRM value is \(\beta_N^2\). The Yuen--Kennedy--Lax dual operator

\[
Y=\frac{\beta_N}{N}\sqrt{C_{N,i}}
\]

is feasible because, with \(S=\sqrt{C_{N,i}}\),

\[
\beta_NS-Se_je_j^*S\ge0.
\]

Indeed, since \(S\succ0\), congruence by \(S^{-1/2}\) reduces this to

\[
\beta_N I-S^{1/2}e_je_j^*S^{1/2}\ge0.
\]

The rank-one term has unique nonzero eigenvalue \(e_j^*Se_j=\beta_N\), because the diagonal of \(S\) is constant. Since \(\operatorname{tr}Y=\beta_N^2\), the SRM is exactly optimal for the circulant model.

Let

\[
\tau_N=\|\sqrt{G_{N,i}}-\sqrt{C_{N,i}}\|_1.
\]

Put \(\Delta_N=\sqrt{G_{N,i}}-\sqrt{C_{N,i}}\). The canonical Gram realizations, followed by Cauchy--Schwarz and the monotonicity of Schatten norms, give the explicit chain

\begin{align}\label{eq:opt-circulant-transfer}
|P_{\rm opt}(G_{N,i})-P_{\rm opt}(C_{N,i})|
&\le\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N\|\Delta_Ne_j\|_2 \notag\\
&\le\frac1{\sqrt N}
\left(\sum_{j=1}^N\|\Delta_Ne_j\|_2^2\right)^{1/2} \notag\\
&=\frac{\|\Delta_N\|_{\rm HS}}{\sqrt N}
\notag\\
&\le\frac{\|\Delta_N\|_1}{\sqrt N}
=\frac{\tau_N}{\sqrt N}.
\end{align}

while the SRM diagonal formula gives

\[
|P_{\rm SRM}(G_{N,i})-P_{\rm SRM}(C_{N,i})|
\le\frac{2\tau_N}{N}.
\]

Together with \eqref{eq:sqrt-trace-perturbation}, this proves \eqref{eq:fixed-gap-rate}. Finally, the sampled eigenvalues form a Riemann sum. The rank-one uniform-mode correction changes \(N^{-1}\operatorname{tr}\sqrt C\) by at most \(O(N^{-1/2})\), and therefore

\[
\frac1N\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{C_{N,i}}
\longrightarrow
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{f_{i,r}(\theta)}\,d\theta.
\]

This proves \eqref{eq:fixed-limit}--\eqref{eq:fixed-integral} for \(0<r<1\). The cases \(r=0,1\) are handled below.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Residual Toeplitz family]\label{cor:residual-toeplitz}
Fix \(i\ge1\) and \(0\le r<1\), put \(q=r^i\), and let

\[
R_{N,i}:=\frac{G_{N,i}-qJ_N}{1-q}
=T_N\!\left(\frac{f_{i,r}}{1-r^i}\right).
\]

Then \(R_{N,i}\) is the Gram matrix of normalized residual states and

\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual-toeplitz-limit}
\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{\rm opt}(R_{N,i})
=\lim_{N\to\infty}P_{\rm SRM}(R_{N,i})
=\frac{P_\infty(i,r)}{1-r^i}.
\end{equation}

Equivalently,
\((1-r^i)P_{\rm opt}(R_{N,i})\to P_\infty(i,r)\), and the same statement
holds with \(P_{\rm SRM}\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The diagonal of \(G_{N,i}-qJ_N\) is \(1-q\), and
\eqref{eq:residual-spectral-gap} shows that it is positive definite. Thus
\(R_{N,i}\) is a unit-diagonal Gram matrix. Its Toeplitz symbol is
\(h_{i,r}=f_{i,r}/(1-r^i)\), which is bounded above and below away from zero.
The one-dimensional instance of Lemma~\ref{lem:folner-diagonal}, followed by
Lemma~\ref{lem:gram-upper-bound} and \eqref{eq:trace-srm-bound}, gives the common
limit

\[
\left[\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{h_{i,r}(\theta)}\,d\theta\right]^2
=\frac{P_\infty(i,r)}{1-r^i}.
\]
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exact and special-function cases}\label{exact-and-special-function-cases}

\begin{corollary}[One-site interval, finite size]\label{cor:one-site}
For \(i=1\),

\[
G_{N,1}=(1-r)I_N+rJ_N
\]

is permutation invariant. Its positive square root has constant diagonal, so the preceding dual certificate proves finite-size SRM optimality. Since its eigenvalues are \(1+(N-1)r\) once and \(1-r\) with multiplicity \(N-1\),

\begin{align}\label{eq:one-site-finite}
P_{\rm opt}(G_{N,1})
&=P_{\rm SRM}(G_{N,1}) \notag\\
&=\frac{\bigl[\sqrt{1+(N-1)r}
+(N-1)\sqrt{1-r}\bigr]^2}{N^2}.
\end{align}

In particular, \(P_\infty(1,r)=1-r\).

This is the \(k=1\) specialization of the known single-anomaly result in
Refs.~\cite{skotiniotis2024,llorens2024}, after identifying their anomaly
overlap parameter with \(r=c^2\). Its inclusion records how the interval
family recovers the established finite-size formula at \(i=1\).
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Two-site interval]\label{cor:two-site}
With

\[
E(m)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1-m\sin^2u}\,du,
\]

one has

\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-site-elliptic}
P_\infty(2,r)
=\frac4{\pi^2}(1-r)(1+3r)
E^2\!\left(\frac{4r}{1+3r}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}[Derivation]
Equation~\eqref{eq:fixed-symbol} becomes

\begin{align*}
f_{2,r}(\theta)
&=(1-r)(1+r+2r\cos\theta) \\
&=(1-r)(1+3r-4r\sin^2(\theta/2)).
\end{align*}

With \(u=\theta/2\) and symmetry about \(\pi/2\),

\begin{align*}
&\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{f_{2,r}(\theta)}\,d\theta \\
&\qquad=\frac2\pi\sqrt{(1-r)(1+3r)}
E\!\left(\frac{4r}{1+3r}\right).
\end{align*}

Squaring proves \eqref{eq:two-site-elliptic}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Endpoint parameters}\label{endpoint-parameters}

If \(r=0\), then \(G_{N,i}=I_N\) and both success probabilities equal one. If \(r=1\), then \(G_{N,i}=J_N\), all hypotheses represent the same ray, and both probabilities equal \(1/N\). These values agree with the continuous limiting formulas. All spectral-gap arguments above are used only for \(0<r<1\).

\section{A growing known length}\label{a-growing-known-length}

Define the Poisson kernel

\[
g_r(\theta)=\sum_{d\in\mathbb Z}r^{|d|}e^{id\theta}
=\frac{1-r^2}{1-2r\cos\theta+r^2}
\]

and

\begin{equation}\label{eq:p1-definition}
p_1(r)=\left[\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{g_r(\theta)}\,d\theta\right]^2
=\frac{4(1-r^2)}{\pi^2}K^2(r^2),
\end{equation}

where \(K\) is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind in the parameter convention and \(p_1(1)=0\) by continuity. The last equality follows from the standard Landen transformation; the integral representation is the definition used in all proofs.

\begin{proposition}[One-change Toeplitz benchmark]\label{prop:one-change-benchmark}
For

\[
Q_N=(r^{|a-b|})_{a,b=1}^N,
\qquad 0\le r<1,
\]

one has

\begin{align}\label{eq:one-change-limit}
P_{\rm opt}(Q_N),P_{\rm SRM}(Q_N)
&\longrightarrow p_1(r), \notag\\
\frac1N\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{Q_N}
&\longrightarrow\sqrt{p_1(r)}.
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The matrix \(Q_N\) is the compression of the one-dimensional convolution operator with kernel \(r^{|d|}\), whose symbol is \(g_r\) and is bounded above and below away from zero. Lemma~\ref{lem:folner-diagonal}, with \(d=1\) and \(\phi(t)=\sqrt t\), gives convergence in mean of the square-root diagonal to \((2\pi)^{-1}\int\sqrt{g_r}=\sqrt{p_1(r)}\). Lemma~\ref{lem:gram-upper-bound} supplies the matching minimum-error upper bound, while \eqref{eq:trace-srm-bound} supplies the lower bound and the SRM squeeze. This also recovers the benchmark of Ref. \cite{sentis2016}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Long known interval]\label{thm:long-known}
Let \(i=i_n\) and \(N_n=n-i_n+1\). If

\begin{equation}\label{eq:long-known-conditions}
i_n\to\infty,\qquad N_n\to\infty,
\end{equation}

then, for every fixed \(0\le r\le1\),

\begin{equation}\label{eq:long-known-limit}
P_{\rm opt}(G_{N_n,i_n}),
P_{\rm SRM}(G_{N_n,i_n})
\longrightarrow p_1(r)=p_1(c^2).
\end{equation}

No condition on \(i_n/N_n\) is required.
The convergence is pointwise in fixed \(r\). No finite-size rate or
uniformity as \(r\uparrow1\) is claimed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The endpoint values \(r=0,1\) follow from the matrices described in
Section~\ref{endpoint-parameters}. We therefore assume \(0<r<1\) and put
\(q_i=r^i\). Separating the common all-zero component gives

\[
|\Phi_a^{(i)}\rangle
=\sqrt{q_i}|\Omega\rangle
+\sqrt{1-q_i}|\xi_a^{(i)}\rangle,
\qquad \langle\Omega|\xi_a^{(i)}\rangle=0.
\]

The normalized residual Gram matrix is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual-gram}
(R_{N,i})_{ab}=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{r^{|a-b|}-r^i}{1-r^i},&|a-b|<i,\\
0,&|a-b|\ge i.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Its symbol

\[
h_{i,r}(\theta)
=1+2\sum_{d=1}^{i-1}\frac{r^d-r^i}{1-r^i}\cos(d\theta)
\]

satisfies

\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual-symbol-convergence}
\|h_{i,r}-g_r\|_\infty
\le\frac{2(i-1)r^i}{1-r^i}+\frac{2r^i}{1-r}
=:\varepsilon_i(r)\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}

Let \(Q_N=(r^{|a-b|})_{a,b=1}^N\). Toeplitz compression gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:residual-operator-convergence}
\|R_{N,i}-Q_N\|_{\rm op}\le\varepsilon_i(r).
\end{equation}

Since \(g_r\ge(1-r)/(1+r)>0\), both matrices have a common positive
spectral gap for large \(i\). Lemma~\ref{lem:gram-stability} therefore
transfers the minimum-error optimum uniformly in \(N\). The trace distance
between each original state and its residual state is also
\(\sqrt{q_i}=r^{i/2}\), so

\[
|P_{\rm opt}(G_{N,i})-P_{\rm opt}(R_{N,i})|
\le r^{i/2}.
\]

The single-change-point Toeplitz limit for \(Q_N\) therefore proves

\[
P_{\rm opt}(G_{N,i})\to p_1(r).
\]

For the SRM, the rank-one common component requires a separate trace estimate.
Since

\[
G_{N,i}=q_iJ_N+(1-q_i)R_{N,i},
\]

operator monotonicity and the Rotfel'd trace inequality give the exact sandwich

\begin{align}\label{eq:rank-one-sandwich}
\sqrt{1-q_i}\,\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{R_{N,i}}
&\le\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{G_{N,i}} \notag\\
&\le\sqrt{1-q_i}\,\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{R_{N,i}}
+\sqrt{q_iN}.
\end{align}

Equation~\eqref{eq:residual-operator-convergence}, the common spectral gap, and the Toeplitz limit imply

\[
\frac1N\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{R_{N,i}}
\longrightarrow\sqrt{p_1(r)}.
\]

After dividing \eqref{eq:rank-one-sandwich} by \(N\), the remaining rank-one term is \(\sqrt{q_i/N}\to0\). Thus

\[
\frac1N\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{G_{N,i}}
\longrightarrow\sqrt{p_1(r)}.
\]

The sandwich \eqref{eq:trace-srm-bound} completes the SRM proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[An exact finite-size regime]\label{rem:exact-finite-regime}
If \(i\ge N-1\), then \(\min(|a-b|,i)=|a-b|\) for every matrix entry, and hence

\[
G_{N,i}=Q_N
\]

exactly. The approximation in Theorem~\ref{thm:long-known} is needed only when \(i<N-1\).
\end{remark}

\section{Unknown interval length}\label{unknown-interval-length}

Let

\begin{align*}
\mathcal I_n&=\{[a,b]:1\le a\le b\le n\}, \\
M_n&=|\mathcal I_n|=\frac{n(n+1)}2,
\end{align*}

and equip \(\mathcal I_n\) with the uniform prior \(1/M_n\). This is uniform
over intervals, not over lengths. If \(L=|I|\), the induced marginal is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:induced-length-prior}
\begin{aligned}
\Pr\{L=\ell\}
&=\frac{n-\ell+1}{M_n}\\
&=\frac{2(n-\ell+1)}{n(n+1)},
\qquad 1\leq\ell\leq n.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Conditionally on \(L=\ell\), the starting point is uniform over its
\(n-\ell+1\) admissible values. Consequently
\(\mathbb E L=(n+2)/3\), and \(L/n\) converges to the triangular density
\(2(1-x)\) on \(0<x<1\). Thus the prior favors shorter lengths, but its
typical interval still has order-\(n\) length. The candidate indexed by
\(I=[a,b]\) has \(|\psi\rangle\) on \(I\) and \(|0\rangle\) elsewhere. Its
exact Gram kernel is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:interval-gram}
G^{(n)}_{I,J}=c^{|I\triangle J|}.
\end{equation}

Theorem~\ref{thm:unknown-length} is therefore a Bayes law for the
uniform-over-intervals prior in \eqref{eq:induced-length-prior}. Alternative
priors may retain nonvanishing mass on bounded or logarithmically short
intervals and lead to different limiting reductions.

For intersecting intervals,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:intersecting-distance}
|I\triangle J|=|a-a'|+|b-b'|.
\end{equation}

For disjoint intervals, assume without loss of generality that \(b<a'\) and define the separating gap by

\[
g(I,J):=a'-b-1\ge0.
\]

Then the exact identity is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:disjoint-distance}
|a-a'|+|b-b'|=|I\triangle J|+2g(I,J).
\end{equation}

Thus \eqref{eq:intersecting-distance} still holds for adjacent intervals, where \(g(I,J)=0\), but fails by twice the separating gap when \(g(I,J)>0\). The exact physical kernel is therefore not globally a two-dimensional Toeplitz kernel.

Classical multilevel Szeg{\H o} theorems describe spectral averages for genuine
multilevel Toeplitz compressions \cite{tilli1998,tyrtyshnikov1998}. Here the
triangular endpoint sets and, more importantly, the non-Toeplitz physical
kernel require the following local-diagonal Følner statement together with a
separate perturbative transfer back to \(G^{(n)}\).

\subsection{A Følner functional-calculus lemma}\label{a-folner-functional-calculus-lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Local diagonal Følner limit]\label{lem:folner-diagonal}
Let \(k\in\ell^1(\mathbb Z^d)\) satisfy \(k(-u)=\overline{k(u)}\), let \(A\) be convolution by \(k\) on \(\ell^2(\mathbb Z^d)\), and suppose its Fourier symbol satisfies

\[
0<\mu\le F(\theta)\le L<\infty
\]

almost everywhere. If \(\Lambda_n\subset\mathbb Z^d\) is any finite Følner sequence and \(A_n=P_nAP_n|_{\ell^2(\Lambda_n)}\), then for every continuous \(\phi:[\mu,L]\to\mathbb C\),

\begin{equation}\label{eq:folner-diagonal}
\frac1{|\Lambda_n|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n}
\left|
\langle\delta_x,\phi(A_n)\delta_x\rangle-a_\phi
\right|\longrightarrow0,
\end{equation}

where

\[
a_\phi=\frac1{(2\pi)^d}\int_{[0,2\pi]^d}\phi(F(\theta))\,d\theta.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since \(A\) has spectrum in \([\mu,L]\), the same is true of every compression
\(A_n\). Fix \(\varepsilon>0\) and put \(\eta=\varepsilon/8\). By the
Weierstrass theorem, applied to the real and imaginary parts if necessary,
choose a polynomial

\[
p(t)=\sum_{m=0}^{s}a_mt^m
\quad\text{such that}\quad
\sup_{t\in[\mu,L]}|\phi(t)-p(t)|<\eta.
\]

Let \(k_R=k\,\mathbf 1_{\{\|u\|_\infty\le R\}}\), let \(A_R\) be convolution
by \(k_R\), and set \(A_{R,n}=P_nA_RP_n|_{\ell^2(\Lambda_n)}\). Young's
inequality gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:folner-young}
\|A-A_R\|_{\rm op},\ \|A_n-A_{R,n}\|_{\rm op}
\le\|k-k_R\|_{\ell^1}.
\end{equation}

Choose \(R\) so large that the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:folner-young} is at most \(1\) and

\[
C_p\|k-k_R\|_{\ell^1}<\eta,\qquad
C_p:=\sum_{m=1}^{s}m|a_m|(L+1)^{m-1},
\]

where the second condition is void if \(C_p=0\). The telescoping identity for
\(X^m-Y^m\), together with
\(\|A\|,\|A_n\|\le L\) and
\(\|A_R\|,\|A_{R,n}\|\le L+1\), then yields

\begin{equation}\label{eq:folner-polynomial-truncation}
\|p(A)-p(A_R)\|_{\rm op},
\ \|p(A_n)-p(A_{R,n})\|_{\rm op}<\eta.
\end{equation}

Define

\[
Q_t=\{h\in\mathbb Z^d:\|h\|_\infty\le t\}
\]

and the \(sR\)-interior and boundary by

\begin{align*}
\Lambda_n^{\circ,sR}
&=\{x\in\Lambda_n:x+Q_{sR}\subseteq\Lambda_n\},\\
\partial_{sR}\Lambda_n
&=\Lambda_n\setminus\Lambda_n^{\circ,sR}.
\end{align*}

Every length-\(m\le s\) path generated by the range-\(R\) kernel and starting
at \(x\in\Lambda_n^{\circ,sR}\) stays in \(\Lambda_n\). Therefore

\begin{equation}\label{eq:folner-interior-equality}
\langle\delta_x,p(A_{R,n})\delta_x\rangle
=\langle\delta_0,p(A_R)\delta_0\rangle,
\qquad x\in\Lambda_n^{\circ,sR}.
\end{equation}

The radius here is \(sR\), not \(R\). Moreover,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:folner-boundary-inclusion}
\partial_{sR}\Lambda_n
\subseteq
\bigcup_{\|h\|_\infty\le sR}
\bigl(\Lambda_n\setminus(\Lambda_n-h)\bigr).
\end{equation}

The union is finite and each summand has \(o(|\Lambda_n|)\) elements by the
Følner property. Hence
\(|\partial_{sR}\Lambda_n|/|\Lambda_n|\to0\).
With
\(K_p=\sup_{|t|\le L+1}|p(t)|\), define

\begin{align*}
d_{n,R,p}(x)
&:=
\left|
\langle\delta_x,p(A_{R,n})\delta_x\rangle
-\langle\delta_0,p(A_R)\delta_0\rangle
\right|,\\
D_{n,R,p}
&:=\frac1{|\Lambda_n|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n}d_{n,R,p}(x).
\end{align*}

Equations
\eqref{eq:folner-interior-equality}--\eqref{eq:folner-boundary-inclusion} give

\begin{equation}\label{eq:folner-boundary-average}
D_{n,R,p}
\le2K_p\frac{|\partial_{sR}\Lambda_n|}{|\Lambda_n|}
\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}

Finally, the spectral theorem and Fourier transformation identify

\[
\langle\delta_0,\phi(A)\delta_0\rangle
=\frac1{(2\pi)^d}\int_{[0,2\pi]^d}\phi(F(\theta))\,d\theta
=a_\phi.
\]

Put

\[
D_n^\phi:=\frac1{|\Lambda_n|}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_n}
\left|\langle\delta_x,\phi(A_n)\delta_x\rangle-a_\phi\right|.
\]

For all sufficiently large \(n\), functional calculus,
\eqref{eq:folner-polynomial-truncation}, and
\eqref{eq:folner-boundary-average} now give the single uniform error chain

\begin{align*}
D_n^\phi
&\le
\|\phi(A_n)-p(A_n)\|_{\rm op}\\
&\quad+\|p(A_n)-p(A_{R,n})\|_{\rm op}\\
&\quad+D_{n,R,p}\\
&\quad+\|p(A_R)-p(A)\|_{\rm op}\\
&\quad+\|p(A)-\phi(A)\|_{\rm op}\\
&<4\eta+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.
\end{align*}

This proves \eqref{eq:folner-diagonal}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Gram upper bound for equiprobable pure states]\label{lem:gram-upper-bound}
Let \(G\) be an \(M\times M\) positive semidefinite Gram matrix with unit diagonal. Set

\[
d_j=(\sqrt G)_{jj},\qquad
q_j=\frac{d_j}{\operatorname{tr}\sqrt G},\qquad
u_j=\frac1M.
\]

Then

\begin{align}\label{eq:gram-upper-bound}
\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}\sqrt G}{M}\right)^2
&\le P_{\rm opt}(G) \notag\\
&\le
\left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}\sqrt G}{M}\right)^2
+\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(G)}\,\|q-u\|_1.
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The lower bound is \eqref{eq:trace-srm-bound}. For \(G\succ0\), the upper
bound is Theorem 1 of Ref.~\cite{sentis2016}, rewritten in our normalization
so that no prior-weight or hypothesis-count factor is implicit. That theorem
assumes linearly independent states, equivalently a positive definite Gram
matrix. For singular \(G\), apply it to

\[
G_\varepsilon=(1-\varepsilon)G+\varepsilon I_M,
\qquad 0<\varepsilon<1.
\]

Each \(G_\varepsilon\) is positive definite and has unit diagonal. As \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\), the positive square root, its trace and diagonal, \(\lambda_{\max}\), and hence \(q\) all converge to their counterparts for \(G\). Lemma~\ref{lem:gram-stability} also gives \(P_{\rm opt}(G_\varepsilon)\to P_{\rm opt}(G)\). Passing to the limit proves the singular case.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Exceptional-sector Gram transfer]
\label{thm:exceptional-sector-transfer}
For each \(n\), let \(G_n\) be an \(M_n\times M_n\) positive semidefinite
Gram matrix with unit diagonal. Let \(\mathcal B_n\) be a set of \(m_n\)
labels, let \(G_{n,\mathcal B}\) be the associated principal submatrix, and
let \(H_n\) be an \(m_n\times m_n\) positive semidefinite matrix with unit
diagonal. Suppose

\begin{align}
\frac{m_n}{M_n}&\longrightarrow1,
\label{eq:transfer-mass}\\
\|G_{n,\mathcal B}-H_n\|_{\rm op}&\longrightarrow0,
\label{eq:transfer-gram}\\
\sup_n\|H_n\|_{\rm op}&<\infty,
\label{eq:transfer-spectral-bound}
\end{align}

and, for some \(\alpha>0\),

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transfer-local-diagonal}
\frac1{m_n}\sum_{j=1}^{m_n}
\left|(\sqrt{H_n})_{jj}-\alpha\right|\longrightarrow0.
\end{equation}

Then the complete equiprobable ensemble satisfies

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transfer-conclusion}
P_{\rm opt}(G_n),P_{\rm SRM}(G_n)\longrightarrow\alpha^2.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put \(d_{n,j}=(\sqrt{H_n})_{jj}\) and
\(\bar d_n=m_n^{-1}\sum_jd_{n,j}\). Positivity and the unit diagonal give
\(0\le d_{n,j}\le1\), because

\[
d_{n,j}^2
\le\langle e_j,H_ne_j\rangle=1.
\]

Equation~\eqref{eq:transfer-local-diagonal} implies
\(\bar d_n\to\alpha\) and

\[
\frac1{m_n}\sum_jd_{n,j}^2\longrightarrow\alpha^2.
\]

The latter is \(P_{\rm SRM}(H_n)\). To control the optimum, define

\[
q_{n,j}=\frac{d_{n,j}}{m_n\bar d_n},
\qquad u_{n,j}=\frac1{m_n}.
\]

For all sufficiently large \(n\), \(\bar d_n>0\), and

\begin{align*}
\|q_n-u_n\|_1
&=\frac1{\bar d_n}
\frac1{m_n}\sum_j|d_{n,j}-\bar d_n|\\
&\le
\frac{1}{m_n\bar d_n}\sum_j|d_{n,j}-\alpha|
+\frac{|\bar d_n-\alpha|}{\bar d_n}
\longrightarrow0.
\end{align*}

Lemma~\ref{lem:gram-upper-bound}, together with
\eqref{eq:transfer-spectral-bound}, now gives

\[
P_{\rm opt}(H_n)\longrightarrow\alpha^2.
\]

By Lemma~\ref{lem:gram-stability} and
\eqref{eq:transfer-gram}, the retained physical family has the same limits:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transfer-retained-limits}
P_{\rm opt}(G_{n,\mathcal B}),
P_{\rm SRM}(G_{n,\mathcal B})\longrightarrow\alpha^2.
\end{equation}

The same square-root perturbation bound also yields

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transfer-retained-trace}
\frac{\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{G_{n,\mathcal B}}}{m_n}
\longrightarrow\alpha.
\end{equation}

Write \(\beta_n=m_n/M_n\). Restricting a full-ensemble POVM to the retained
labels and completing the resulting sub-POVM gives the upper bound below;
extending an optimal retained-family POVM gives the lower bound:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transfer-optimum-sandwich}
\beta_nP_{\rm opt}(G_{n,\mathcal B})
\le P_{\rm opt}(G_n)
\le\beta_nP_{\rm opt}(G_{n,\mathcal B})+(1-\beta_n).
\end{equation}

Equations~\eqref{eq:transfer-mass},
\eqref{eq:transfer-retained-limits}, and
\eqref{eq:transfer-optimum-sandwich} prove
\(P_{\rm opt}(G_n)\to\alpha^2\).

It remains to transfer the SRM. Put

\[
t_n=\frac{\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{G_n}}{M_n}.
\]

Eigenvalue interlacing for the principal submatrix gives

\[
t_n\ge
\beta_n\frac{\operatorname{tr}\sqrt{G_{n,\mathcal B}}}{m_n}
\longrightarrow\alpha.
\]

On the other hand, \(t_n^2\le P_{\rm opt}(G_n)\to\alpha^2\), so
\(t_n\to\alpha\). The universal squeeze

\[
t_n^2\le P_{\rm SRM}(G_n)\le P_{\rm opt}(G_n)
\]

proves \eqref{eq:transfer-conclusion}.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Følner comparison transfer]
\label{cor:folner-comparison-transfer}
Let \(k\in\ell^1(\mathbb Z^d)\) be Hermitian with \(k(0)=1\), and suppose
the symbol \(F\) of its convolution operator satisfies
\(0<\mu\le F\le L<\infty\). Let \(\Lambda_n\) be a finite Følner sequence
and \(H_n\) the compression of this convolution operator to
\(\ell^2(\Lambda_n)\). If \(G_n\) and retained label sets
\(\mathcal B_n\) satisfy

\[
\frac{|\mathcal B_n|}{M_n}\to1,
\qquad
\|G_n[\mathcal B_n]-H_n\|_{\rm op}\to0,
\]

define

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:folner-transfer-alpha}
\alpha_F=\frac1{(2\pi)^d}
\int_{[0,2\pi]^d}\sqrt{F(\theta)}\,d\theta.
\end{equation}

Then

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:folner-transfer-conclusion}
P_{\rm opt}(G_n),P_{\rm SRM}(G_n)
\longrightarrow\alpha_F^2.
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The matrices \(H_n\) have unit diagonal and
\(\|H_n\|_{\rm op}\le L\). Lemma~\ref{lem:folner-diagonal}, applied to
\(\phi(t)=\sqrt t\), gives
\eqref{eq:transfer-local-diagonal} with

\[
\alpha=\alpha_F>0.
\]

Theorem~\ref{thm:exceptional-sector-transfer} applies.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The two-endpoint law}\label{the-two-endpoint-law}

\begin{theorem}[Unknown-length interval]\label{thm:unknown-length}
Under the uniform prior on \(\mathcal I_n\),

\begin{equation}\label{eq:unknown-length-limit}
P_{\rm opt}(G^{(n)}),P_{\rm SRM}(G^{(n)})
\longrightarrow p_1(c)^2
\end{equation}

for every fixed \(0\le c\le1\).
The convergence is pointwise in fixed \(c\). No finite-size rate or
uniformity as \(c\uparrow1\) is claimed.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(c=0\), the hypotheses are orthogonal, whereas for \(c=1\) they are
identical; both cases agree with the stated limit. Assume henceforth that
\(0<c<1\), and choose

\[
L_n=\lceil\gamma\log n\rceil,
\qquad \gamma>|\log c|^{-1},
\]

and retain only

\[
\mathcal B_n=\{I\in\mathcal I_n:|I|\ge L_n\}.
\]

The deleted prior mass is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:short-interval-mass}
\varepsilon_n
=1-\frac{|\mathcal B_n|}{M_n}
=O\!\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)\to0.
\end{equation}

On \(\mathcal B_n\), define the comparison kernel

\begin{equation}\label{eq:comparison-kernel}
H^{(n)}_{I,J}=c^{|a-a'|+|b-b'|}.
\end{equation}

It is the compression of the positive convolution kernel \(c^{|u|+|v|}\). The two kernels agree for intersecting intervals. For disjoint intervals,

\[
|G_{I,J}-H_{I,J}|
\le c^{|I|+|J|}\le c^{2L_n}.
\]

Consequently,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:comparison-operator}
\|G_{\mathcal B_n}-H_{\mathcal B_n}\|_{\rm op}
\le|\mathcal B_n|c^{2L_n}=o(1).
\end{equation}

For completeness, the required Følner property follows directly. Under the endpoint
identification \(I=[a,b]\leftrightarrow(a,b)\),

\[
\mathcal B_n
=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb Z^2:a\ge1,\ b\le n,\ b-a\ge L_n-1\},
\]

and

\begin{equation}\label{eq:long-domain-cardinality}
|\mathcal B_n|
=\frac{(n-L_n+1)(n-L_n+2)}2
\sim\frac{n^2}{2}.
\end{equation}

Fix \(h=(u,v)\in\mathbb Z^2\) and put \(H=\|h\|_\infty\). If the indicators
of \(\mathcal B_n\) at \(x=(a,b)\) and \(x+h\) differ, at least one of the
three affine inequalities defining \(\mathcal B_n\) changes truth value.
Such an \(x\) lies in a strip of respectively \(|u|\), \(|v|\), or
\(|v-u|\) lattice layers adjacent to
\(a=1\), \(b=n\), or \(b-a=L_n-1\). Because either \(x\) or \(x+h\) belongs
to \(\mathcal B_n\), each layer contains at most \(n+2H+1\) relevant points.
Put \(C_h=|u|+|v|+|v-u|\). Therefore

\begin{equation}\label{eq:long-domain-folner-bound}
\frac{|(\mathcal B_n-h)\triangle\mathcal B_n|}{|\mathcal B_n|}
\le
\frac{C_h(n+2H+1)}{|\mathcal B_n|}
\longrightarrow0,
\end{equation}

where \eqref{eq:long-domain-cardinality} and \(L_n=O(\log n)=o(n)\) were
used. Thus \((\mathcal B_n)\) is a Følner sequence in \(\mathbb Z^2\).
The comparison convolution symbol is

\[
F_c(\theta_1,\theta_2)=g_c(\theta_1)g_c(\theta_2),
\]

with a uniform positive lower bound. The square-root spectral average in
Corollary~\ref{cor:folner-comparison-transfer} factorizes as

\begin{align}\label{eq:endpoint-factorization}
\alpha_c
&=\frac1{(2\pi)^2}\int\!\!\int\sqrt{F_c} \notag\\
&=\left[\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}
\sqrt{g_c(\theta)}\,d\theta\right]^2
=p_1(c).
\end{align}

The kernel \(k(u,v)=c^{|u|+|v|}\) is Hermitian, belongs to
\(\ell^1(\mathbb Z^2)\), and has \(k(0,0)=1\). Equations
\eqref{eq:short-interval-mass}, \eqref{eq:comparison-operator}, and
\eqref{eq:long-domain-folner-bound} verify all remaining hypotheses of
Corollary~\ref{cor:folner-comparison-transfer}. That corollary transfers the
comparison-kernel limit simultaneously to the optimum and SRM of the full,
untruncated physical Gram family, giving
\(\alpha_c^2=p_1(c)^2\).
\end{proof}

\section{Joint detection and exact localization}\label{joint-detection-and-exact-localization}

Add the no-anomaly state

\[
|\Omega\rangle=|0\rangle^{\otimes n}
\]

with fixed prior \(\pi_0\in(0,1)\). No-change labels already appear in the
permanent- and at-most-change formulations of
Refs.~\cite{sentis2016,mohan2023}; the result below concerns a returning
interval and a no-change prior that remains nonzero as \(n\to\infty\).

For known length, the complete joint prior is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:h0-known-joint-prior}
\Pr(H_0)=\pi_0,
\qquad
\Pr(a)=\frac{1-\pi_0}{N},
\quad 1\le a\le N.
\end{equation}

For unknown length, it is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:h0-unknown-joint-prior}
\Pr(H_0)=\pi_0,
\qquad
\Pr(I)=\frac{1-\pi_0}{M_n},
\quad I\in\mathcal I_n.
\end{equation}

Thus the anomaly label is conditionally uniform given that an anomaly is
present. The task is successful only if the measurement reports \(H_0\) for
\(|\Omega\rangle\), or reports the exact anomalous interval otherwise. We
write \(P_{\rm joint}^*\) for the Bayes optimum over all POVMs. The results in
this section concern this optimum; they do not assert asymptotic optimality of
the weighted SRM for the augmented, nonuniform ensemble.

\begin{theorem}[Fixed known length with no-anomaly prior]\label{thm:h0-known-length}
Under the joint prior \eqref{eq:h0-known-joint-prior}, for fixed \(i\),

\begin{equation}\label{eq:h0-fixed-limit}
P_{\rm joint}^*(N,i,r,\pi_0)
\longrightarrow
\pi_0+(1-\pi_0)P_\infty(i,r).
\end{equation}

If \(i=i_n\to\infty\) and \(N_n\to\infty\), then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:h0-growing-limit}
P_{\rm joint}^*
\longrightarrow
\pi_0+(1-\pi_0)p_1(c^2).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If \(r=1\), every anomalous candidate equals \(|\Omega\rangle\). The \(N+1\)
labels are therefore represented by one physical state, with label priors
\(\pi_0\) and \((1-\pi_0)/N\). Hence, exactly at finite \(N\),

\begin{equation}\label{eq:h0-fixed-r-one}
P_{\rm joint}^*(N,i,1,\pi_0)
=\max\!\left\{\pi_0,\frac{1-\pi_0}{N}\right\}
\longrightarrow\pi_0.
\end{equation}

This agrees with \eqref{eq:h0-fixed-limit} because
\(P_\infty(i,1)=0\). Assume henceforth that \(0\le r<1\).
Put \(q=r^i\) and use

\[
|\Phi_a^{(i)}\rangle
=\sqrt q|\Omega\rangle+\sqrt{1-q}|\xi_a^{(i)}\rangle.
\]

If \(P_G^*\) and \(P_R^*\) are the equal-prior localization optima for the original and residual ensembles, respectively, then the explicit measurement that assigns \(|\Omega\rangle\) to \(H_0\) and uses an optimal residual measurement on \(|\Omega\rangle^\perp\) gives

\[
\pi_0+(1-\pi_0)(1-q)P_R^*
\le P_{\rm joint}^*.
\]

Conversely, the no-anomaly contribution is at most \(\pi_0\), and completing the anomalous sub-POVM can only improve its conditional localization score, so

\[
P_{\rm joint}^*\le\pi_0+(1-\pi_0)P_G^*.
\]

For fixed \(i\), Corollary~\ref{cor:residual-toeplitz} gives
\((1-r^i)P_R^*\to P_\infty(i,r)\), and the two bounds prove
\eqref{eq:h0-fixed-limit}. In the growing-length regime,
\(|P_G^*-P_R^*|\le r^{i/2}\) and \(q\to0\); Theorem~\ref{thm:long-known}
then proves \eqref{eq:h0-growing-limit}. At \(r=0\), all \(N+1\) states are
mutually orthogonal and \(P_{\rm joint}^*=1\), also in agreement with both
displayed limits.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Unknown length with no-anomaly prior]\label{thm:h0-unknown-length}
Under the joint prior \eqref{eq:h0-unknown-joint-prior},

\begin{equation}\label{eq:h0-unknown-limit}
P_{\rm joint}^*(n,c,\pi_0)
\longrightarrow
\pi_0+(1-\pi_0)p_1(c)^2.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Assume \(0<c<1\) and use the long-interval family \(\mathcal B_n\) from Theorem~\ref{thm:unknown-length}. For \(I\in\mathcal B_n\), put

\[
s_I=\langle\Omega|\Phi_I\rangle=c^{|I|}\le c^{L_n}=:\eta_n\to0
\]

and

\[
|\xi_I\rangle
=\frac{|\Phi_I\rangle-s_I|\Omega\rangle}{\sqrt{1-s_I^2}}.
\]

To make the conditioning explicit, define the full-family anomalous
localization optimum

\[
P_{G,n}^{\rm all}
:=\max_{\{M_I\}_{I\in\mathcal I_n}}
\frac1{M_n}\sum_{I\in\mathcal I_n}
\langle\Phi_I|M_I|\Phi_I\rangle
=P_{\rm opt}(G^{(n)}),
\]

and define the two equal-prior optima on the retained family by

\begin{align*}
P_{G,n}^{\mathcal B}
&:=\max_{\{M_I\}_{I\in\mathcal B_n}}
\frac1{|\mathcal B_n|}\sum_{I\in\mathcal B_n}
\langle\Phi_I|M_I|\Phi_I\rangle,\\
P_{R,n}^{\mathcal B}
&:=\max_{\{M_I\}_{I\in\mathcal B_n}}
\frac1{|\mathcal B_n|}\sum_{I\in\mathcal B_n}
\langle\xi_I|M_I|\xi_I\rangle.
\end{align*}

In each maximization the operators form a POVM on the support of the
corresponding conditional ensemble, completed arbitrarily on its orthogonal
complement. These are conditional localization probabilities: the factors
\(1-\pi_0\) and \(1-\varepsilon_n\) are not included in their definitions.
The pure-state trace distance gives the uniform comparison

\[
|P_{R,n}^{\mathcal B}-P_{G,n}^{\mathcal B}|\le\eta_n.
\]

Use the optimal residual POVM for the long intervals in \(|\Omega\rangle^\perp\), assign zero operators to deleted short intervals, and assign the remaining operator to \(H_0\). With \(\varepsilon_n=1-|\mathcal B_n|/M_n\), this gives

\[
P_{\rm joint}^*
\ge\pi_0+(1-\pi_0)(1-\varepsilon_n)(1-\eta_n^2)
(P_{G,n}^{\mathcal B}-\eta_n).
\]

The universal upper bound is

\[
P_{\rm joint}^*
\le\pi_0+(1-\pi_0)P_{G,n}^{\rm all}.
\]

Theorem~\ref{thm:unknown-length} and \(\varepsilon_n,\eta_n\to0\) make the bounds coincide. If \(c=0\), all hypotheses are mutually orthogonal and the success probability is one. If \(c=1\), all hypotheses are identical and the optimum is \(\max\{\pi_0,(1-\pi_0)/M_n\}\to\pi_0\).
\end{proof}

\section{Numerical verification}
\label{numerical-verification}

The numerical calculations verify the finite-size Gram matrices and show how
the asymptotic regimes are approached. None of the proofs relies on numerical
evidence.

For small unknown-length instances, we solve the minimum-error problem in
canonical Gram coordinates with both primal and dual semidefinite programs.
The numerical grid covers \(n=3,\ldots,7\) and

\[
c\in\{0.3,0.6,0.8,0.9,0.95,0.99\}.
\]

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/figure3_finite_size.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Finite-size convergence and safeguarded floating-point
  SRM-gap brackets.}
  (a) SRM success for \(i=\lceil\sqrt N\rceil\) and
  \(i=\lfloor N/2\rfloor\); dotted horizontal lines are \(p_1(c^2)\).
  (b) Full dense physical-Gram SRM values through \(n=50\); dotted lines are
  \(p_1(c)^2\), with high-overlap curves retained as stress tests.
  (c) Numerically rechecked endpoints
  \(L_{\rm safe}-P_{\rm SRM}\) and \(U_{\rm safe}-P_{\rm SRM}\) for
  \(n\leq7\). Conditional on exact feasibility, they bracket
  \(P_{\rm opt}-P_{\rm SRM}\); here they are reported as safeguarded
  floating-point diagnostics. Plotting symbols mark only the midpoint of each
  displayed interval, not an estimate of \(P_{\rm opt}-P_{\rm SRM}\); error
  bars are often smaller than the symbols.}
  \label{fig:finite-size}
\end{figure*}

Raw solver objectives are retained as diagnostics but are not used directly as
bounds. Let \(\rho_j\) denote the prior-weighted canonical states on their
\(d_{\rm supp}\)-dimensional support. For a numerical dual matrix \(Y\), set

\[
\delta_D=\max_j\max\{0,-\lambda_{\min}(Y-\rho_j)\}
\]

and add the recorded scale-aware roundoff margin \(\zeta_D>0\). Then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:safe-dual-bound}
Y_{\rm safe}=Y+(\delta_D+\zeta_D)I,\qquad
U_{\rm safe}=\operatorname{tr}Y_{\rm safe}
\end{equation}

is rechecked against every dual PSD constraint. For exact matrices satisfying
those constraints, it supplies a feasible upper bound.

For the primal matrices, project each raw \(M_j\) onto the PSD cone, write the
result as \(M_j^+=B_jB_j^*\), and put \(S=\sum_jM_j^+\). After verifying that
\(S\succ0\) on the \(d_{\rm supp}\)-dimensional canonical support, define

\begin{equation}\label{eq:primal-normalization}
\widetilde M_j
=S^{-1/2}M_j^+S^{-1/2}
=(S^{-1/2}B_j)(S^{-1/2}B_j)^*.
\end{equation}

Then \(\widetilde M_j\succeq0\) and
\(\sum_j\widetilde M_j=I\) in exact arithmetic. In floating point, the
implementation puts \(T=\sum_j\widetilde M_j\), additionally contracts this
sum by

\[
\gamma=\bigl(\max\{1,\lambda_{\max}(T)\}+\zeta_P\bigr)^{-1}
\]

and assigns the PSD remainder \(I-\gamma T\) to one outcome. The resulting
POVM \(\{\widehat M_j\}\) is rechecked. In exact arithmetic, feasibility and
weak duality give

\begin{equation}\label{eq:safe-primal-bound}
L_{\rm safe}=\sum_j\operatorname{tr}(\rho_j\widehat M_j),\qquad
L_{\rm safe}\le P_{\rm opt}\le U_{\rm safe}.
\end{equation}

The implementation, however, uses IEEE double precision and conventional
floating-point eigensolvers, without directed rounding, interval arithmetic,
or exact-rational verification. We therefore report \(L_{\rm safe}\) and
\(U_{\rm safe}\) as safeguarded floating-point bracket endpoints: the displayed
inequality is rigorous conditional on exact feasibility of the postprocessed
matrices, while the stored residuals quantify the numerical recheck at the
working precision.

Across the 30 unknown-length cases, the largest raw absolute primal--dual gap
is \(6.782\times10^{-9}\), while the largest postprocessed floating-point bracket
width \(U_{\rm safe}-L_{\rm safe}\) is \(8.854\times10^{-9}\). The largest
rechecked completeness residual is \(1.38\times10^{-15}\), and every rechecked
primal and dual PSD violation is reported as zero at the evaluator's
resolution. The smallest postprocessed primal
eigenvalue and dual slack eigenvalue are respectively
\(3.4103\times10^{-13}\) and \(3.4102\times10^{-13}\). Full dense
physical-Gram SRM computations for the single-interval
model extend to \(n=50\), with candidate count \(M_n=1275\), over

\[
c\in\{0.5,0.8,0.9,0.95,0.99\}.
\]

For \(n>7\), only \(P_{\rm SRM}\) is computed. The larger-\(n\) calculations
therefore illustrate finite-size SRM convergence; they neither estimate
\(P_{\rm opt}-P_{\rm SRM}\) nor independently establish SRM optimality.

High-overlap cases converge slowly. The correlation length

\[
\xi(c)=|\log c|^{-1}
\]

is approximately \(99.5\) at \(c=0.99\), so even \(n=50\) has
\(n/\xi\approx0.503\). The corresponding finite-size deviations should not be
interpreted as asymptotic counterexamples.

The accompanying reproducibility package records the commands, package
versions, file hashes, solver residuals, and data files. The environment is
pinned to Python 3.12 and fixed versions of NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, CVXPY,
Clarabel, and SCS; the reported clean-environment rerun used Python 3.12.10.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{discussion-and-limitations}

Known and unknown lengths produce different analytic laws. A long known
interval has one translation coordinate, and a unit shift changes two local
factors; its elementary kernel parameter is therefore \(c^2\), giving
\(p_1(c^2)\). An unknown interval has two endpoint coordinates. In the long
intersecting regime, their symmetric-difference distance separates into the
\(\ell^1\) distance of the endpoints, each contributing a kernel with
parameter \(c\); the resulting law is \(p_1(c)^2\). As labeled in
Figure~\ref{fig:analytic-limits}, these formulas correspond to distinct
hypothesis sets and priors. Their juxtaposition expresses the
one-coordinate/two-coordinate geometry rather than a same-prior
value-of-information comparison.

For fixed known length, Theorem~\ref{thm:fixed-length} proves the rigorous rate \(P_{\rm opt}-P_{\rm SRM}=O_{i,r}(N^{-1/2})\). Exploratory finite-size calculations in some fixed-length parameter ranges are consistent with an \(N^{-1}\) decay, but the available computed sizes do not establish a uniform exponent. Determining whether the wrap-around boundary perturbation can be exploited to prove \(O_{i,r}(N^{-1})\), or an asymptotic expansion with a nonzero \(N^{-1}\) coefficient, remains an open problem. By contrast, Theorems~\ref{thm:long-known} and \ref{thm:unknown-length} are qualitative fixed-overlap limits: their proofs give neither a finite-size rate nor bounds uniform in a local regime \(c=c_n\uparrow1\).

The theorems provide collective-measurement benchmarks; an efficient physical
implementation of the optimum remains open. Existing local protocols cover
permanent changes and the \(i=1\) single-anomaly specialization
\cite{sentis2016,llorens2024}. Matched local or sequential optima remain to be
determined for fixed returning intervals with \(i\ge2\), growing known
lengths, and the uniform-over-all-intervals ensemble. Further extensions
include local asymptotic overlaps \(c=c_n\to1\), unknown or mixed anomalous
states, nonuniform or minimax priors, restricted readout, and a growing number
of disjoint intervals. Each changes the hypothesis geometry or admissible
measurement class.

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

A returning quantum change retains enough translation structure for an exact
asymptotic analysis. With fixed known length, the localization probability is
a Toeplitz square-root integral; a growing known length gives the
one-dimensional Toeplitz (single-change) functional evaluated at the effective
compound overlap \(c^2\), namely \(p_1(c^2)\), whereas an unknown length gives
the two-endpoint law \(p_1(c)^2\). The SRM reaches the global minimum-error limit
in all three regimes. The exceptional-sector transfer theorem identifies the
common mechanism carrying a Følner comparison limit to the full physical
ensemble. Under the stated joint priors, a no-change hypothesis changes the
optimal Bayes limit to \(\pi_0+(1-\pi_0)L\); the augmented weighted SRM is not
analyzed. Thus the contiguity constraint turns a general quantum
multiple-hypothesis problem into tractable one- and two-dimensional spectral
limits.
